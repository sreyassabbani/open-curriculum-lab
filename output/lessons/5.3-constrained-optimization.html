Motivating Questions and Objectives

Constrained optimization uses eigenvalues and eigenvectors to find the largest and smallest values of a quadratic form on the unit sphere.

  • How can a quadratic form be optimized when \(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert=1\)?
  • Why do the largest and smallest eigenvalues determine the extreme values?
  • Where are those extreme values attained?
  • How does an orthogonality constraint change the largest allowable value?

After completing the exercises in this topic, you should be able to find constrained maximum and minimum values of a quadratic form and identify where they occur.

Summary of Key Concepts

1. The constrained optimization setting

We want to optimize

\[ Q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x}, \]

where \(A\) is a real symmetric \(n\times n\) matrix. The constraint is

\[ \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert=1. \]

So \(\mathbf{x}\) must lie on the unit sphere. We want the maximum and minimum values of \(Q\), along with the unit vectors where they occur.

Key Idea:

On the unit sphere, the extreme values of \(Q\) are eigenvalues of \(A\). They occur at unit eigenvectors.

2. Extreme Values on the Unit Sphere

Let the real eigenvalues of \(A\) be ordered as

\[ \lambda_1\geq\lambda_2\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_n, \]

Choose corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_n\). When \(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert=1\),

\[ \max Q(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\min Q(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_n. \]

If the largest eigenvalue is repeated, every unit vector in its eigenspace gives the maximum.

Because \(A\) is symmetric, write \(A=PDP^T\), where the columns of \(P\) are \(\mathbf{u}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_n\). Let \(\mathbf{y}=P^T\mathbf{x}\). Then

\[ Q(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i y_i^2, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i^2=1. \]

So the eigenvalues are the weights in a sum of squares whose total is \(1\).

3. Example: a diagonal quadratic form

Consider

\[ Q(\mathbf{x})=9x_1^2+4x_2^2+3x_3^2 \]

subject to \(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert=1\). Here

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}9 & 0 & 0\\0 & 4 & 0\\0 & 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}. \]

The eigenvalues are \(9\), \(4\), and \(3\). The maximum is \(9\) at \(\pm(1,0,0)^T\). The minimum is \(3\) at \(\pm(0,0,1)^T\).

Question 1: Why is \(Q(\mathbf{x})\leq 9\) on the unit sphere?

Click to reveal the answer

Answer: \(9x_1^2+4x_2^2+3x_3^2\leq 9(x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2)=9\).

Explanation: Each coefficient is at most \(9\), and the unit-length constraint makes the sum of the squared coordinates equal to \(1\).

4. Finding the Extremes

  1. Write \(Q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{T}A\mathbf{x}\) and check that \(A\) is symmetric.
  2. Find and order the eigenvalues.
  3. Choose unit eigenvectors for the largest and smallest eigenvalues.
  4. Report the extreme values and where they occur.
Important:

Normalize an eigenvector before using it as a point on the unit sphere.

5. Repeated eigenvalues and orthogonality constraints

In the repeated-eigenvalue example, the eigenvalues are \(1,1,-1\). Since \(A-I\) has one pivot and two free variables, the eigenspace for eigenvalue \(1\) is two-dimensional. Every unit vector in that eigenspace is a maximizer.

A nonzero eigenvector for \(-1\) is \((0,1,1)^T\). The maximizing points form a great circle on the unit sphere, while the minimum occurs at opposite points.

Question 2: What is the dimension of the eigenspace for eigenvalue \(1\)?

Click to reveal the answer

Answer: \(2\).

Explanation: The eigenspace is \(\operatorname{Nul}(A-I)\). Its two free variables give it dimension \(2\).

Question 3: The smallest eigenvalue is \(-1\). Normalize \((0,1,1)^T\), then state the minimum value.

Click to reveal the answer

Answer: The unit eigenvectors are \(\pm(0,1,1)^T/\sqrt{2}\), and the minimum value is \(-1\).

Explanation: The vector has length \(\sqrt{2}\), so dividing by \(\sqrt{2}\) gives a permitted unit vector. The smallest eigenvalue is \(-1\).

Now add the constraint \(\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{u}_1\), along with

\[ \lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u}_1=0. \]

The largest allowed value is now \(\lambda_2\), at \(\mathbf{u}_2\). The minimum is still \(\lambda_n\), at \(\mathbf{u}_n\).

You can add more orthogonality constraints, but this topic uses one.

Question 4: Let \(A=PDP^T\) and \(\mathbf{y}=P^T\mathbf{x}\). If \(\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{u}_1\), what is \(y_1\)? Which largest eigenvalue can still determine \(Q(\mathbf{x})\) on the unit sphere?

Click to reveal the answer

Answer: \(y_1=0\), and the largest remaining eigenvalue is \(\lambda_2\).

Explanation: \(y_1\) measures the component of \(\mathbf{x}\) in the \(\mathbf{u}_1\) direction. Orthogonality removes that direction, so the \(\lambda_1y_1^2\) term is zero.

For example, let

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 0\\0 & 0 & 1\\0 & 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} \]

and require \(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert=1\) and \(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{e}_1=0\). The eigenvalues are \(1,1,-1\). Under these constraints, the maximum is \(1\), at \(\pm(0,1,1)^T/\sqrt{2}\).
